# OUTDOOR LABO Editorial Style Guide

Version: 1.0  
Purpose: Preserve the voice and editorial judgement of OUTDOOR LABO while improving accuracy, readability, discoverability, and publishing efficiency.

## 1. Editorial identity

OUTDOOR LABO is not a catalogue or a product-promotion site. It shares decisions and discoveries made through real use: what prompted the purchase or experiment, what happened in practice, what worked, what did not, and who may benefit from the information.

The central value is **first-hand experience translated into useful judgement**.

Priorities, in order:

1. Accuracy and honesty.
2. First-hand experience and the context behind it.
3. Useful comparisons and practical interpretation.
4. The author's natural voice.
5. Search optimization and presentation.

SEO must never reverse this order.

## 2. Voice and tone

- Write in natural Japanese, usually in the first person.
- Sound like an experienced gear enthusiast talking to an interested reader, not like a manufacturer or an anonymous editorial team.
- Keep technical explanations precise without making the whole article stiff.
- Preserve the author's casual turns of phrase, pauses, asides, and occasional self-deprecating humour when supplied.
- A little looseness is part of the voice. Do not polish every sentence into formal prose.
- Be candid without being needlessly harsh.
- Avoid exaggerated excitement, sales language, empty superlatives, and forced jokes.
- Do not invent catchphrases or imitate the author's mannerisms mechanically.

The desired distance is knowledgeable but approachable: a person who has used a lot of gear and is willing to explain what they actually think.

## 3. The natural starting point

Whenever possible, begin with the reason the subject matters to the author:

- A problem or inconvenience.
- A change in activity, body, environment, or priorities.
- A previous product that no longer fits the need.
- Curiosity about a material, design, technique, or idea.
- A specific use case or experiment.

Do not force a generic introduction such as "This time I will review...". The reader should understand why the author chose to spend time on this subject.

## 4. Evidence and experience

Keep these three kinds of information distinct:

- **Verified facts:** manufacturer specifications, manuals, official announcements, standards, prices, and other sourced information.
- **First-hand observations:** what the author personally bought, tested, measured, noticed, or experienced.
- **Interpretation:** personal preference, evaluation, hypothesis, and conclusions drawn from the experience.

Never present an interpretation as a verified fact. Never claim the author tested something unless the author explicitly says so.

For changing information such as price, availability, model year, firmware, or specifications, verify the current source and note the date or version where useful. Prefer manufacturer documentation for specifications. If reliable confirmation is unavailable, say so or omit the claim.

## 5. Comparisons and evaluation

Comparisons are a core strength of OUTDOOR LABO.

- Bring in products the author has actually owned or used when relevant.
- Explain how a specification affects real use; do not stop at listing numbers.
- Compare in the same context and use case whenever possible.
- Separate product quality from personal fit.
- Do not force a winner when the products serve different needs.
- Explain who may prefer each option.
- Include drawbacks and limitations when known, but do not manufacture a negative point to create artificial balance.

## 6. Structure

Use the structure that best fits the subject. A product review may include background, product overview, reasons for choosing it, actual use, comparisons, strengths, weaknesses, and a conclusion. A DIY or experiment article may instead need materials, dimensions, method, observations, failures, and revisions.

Headings should help readers navigate. Do not add headings just to make an article look substantial. Do not require every article to contain a "pros and cons" section.

## 7. Humour and personality

- Keep humour light, observational, and usually directed at the author's own experience.
- Do not ridicule users, beginners, manufacturers, or people who choose differently.
- Do not make a joke at the expense of a person's body, age, ability, identity, or circumstances.
- Preserve an unusual phrase if it feels natural and belongs to the author.
- Do not add jokes where the subject calls for care or precision.

## 8. SEO and readability

- Reflect the actual search intent in the title and opening.
- Include product names, model numbers, and important terms naturally.
- Use descriptive headings, useful image alt text, and relevant internal links.
- Write a description that accurately summarizes the article; do not promise information the article does not contain.
- Avoid keyword repetition, generic SEO filler, and headings written only for search engines.
- Do not change an existing article slug or URL without an explicit migration decision.

## 9. Editing boundaries

An editor or AI may improve order, clarity, grammar, factual precision, and missing context. It must not:

- Replace personal experience with generic statements.
- Remove meaningful context just to shorten the article.
- flatten distinctive wording into uniform corporate prose.
- Add experiences, measurements, opinions, tests, or conclusions the author did not provide.
- Turn uncertainty into certainty.
- Convert a personal preference into a universal recommendation.
- Rewrite the article as advertising copy.

When a sentence is ambiguous, ask the author rather than guessing.

## 10. Final quality check

Before publication, confirm:

- The reader understands why the author chose the subject.
- First-hand experience is visible and not confused with sourced facts.
- Technical claims are accurate and appropriately sourced.
- Comparisons are fair and relevant.
- Benefits and limitations are represented honestly.
- The conclusion helps readers judge whether the subject fits their own use.
- The author's voice remains recognizable.
- The URL, images, links, taxonomy, and metadata are correct.

**Non-negotiable principle: improve the article without erasing the person who wrote it.**
